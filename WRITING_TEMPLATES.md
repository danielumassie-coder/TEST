# Writing Templates and Examples

## Article Opening Template

### Structure
1. **Hook**: Provocative observation or puzzle (2-3 sentences)
2. **Challenge**: What this challenges in existing theory (2-3 sentences)
3. **Framing**: Your theoretical lens (2-3 sentences)
4. **Contribution preview**: What you'll show (3-4 sentences)

### Example Opening
```
When organizations attempt to solve complex, interdependent problems, they typically 
mobilize frameworks designed to coordinate action: clear roles, defined workflows, 
aligned incentives. Yet these coordination mechanisms often generate precisely the 
"stupid destructive conflicts" they aim to prevent.

This puzzle challenges the dominant framing of collaboration as fundamentally a 
coordination problem. Existing theory treats relational dynamics as secondary to 
structural arrangements, reducing collaborative challenges to information asymmetries 
or misaligned incentives. This atomistic view misses how micro-relational practices 
constitute the very possibility of working together.

Drawing on 18 months of ethnographic observation in cross-sector climate initiatives, 
I reveal how actors build what I term "existential infrastructure"—the underlying 
relational and meaning-making systems that enable collaborative problem-solving. 
Rather than merely coordinating pre-existing interests, actors engage in ongoing 
relational work to establish ontological security, moral coherence, and solidarity.

This study makes three contributions. Empirically, I document micro-practices of 
relational infrastructure building that prior research has not examined. Theoretically, 
I challenge individualistic models of collaboration by showing how micro-relational 
dynamics constitute macro-organizational outcomes. Practically, I provide a framework 
for designing collaborative systems that address existential, not just coordination, 
challenges.
```

## Theory Integration Pattern

### DON'T: Citation list
```
Prior research has examined collaboration (Smith, 2010; Jones, 2012; Lee, 2015; 
Wang, 2018). Studies have also considered conflict (Brown, 2011; Garcia, 2013; 
Martin, 2016). Additionally, scholars have explored trust (Davis, 2009; Wilson, 
2014; Taylor, 2017).
```

### DO: Argumentative integration
```
Dominant approaches to collaboration treat it as a coordination problem (Smith, 2010; 
Jones, 2012): if actors have clear roles, adequate information, and aligned incentives, 
they will work effectively together. This coordination logic, however, cannot explain 
why well-designed collaborative structures often fail (Lee, 2015). I build on emerging 
relational perspectives (Wang, 2018) to argue that collaboration requires not just 
coordination but existential infrastructure—systems that provide ontological security, 
moral coherence, and solidarity.
```

## Empirical Exemplar Structure

### Components
1. **Scene-setting**: Just-enough context (2-3 sentences)
2. **Key moment**: The interaction or event (4-5 sentences)
3. **Theoretical interpretation**: What this shows (2-3 sentences)

### Example
```
Three months into the climate initiative, the technical team and community advocates 
reached an impasse over project metrics. In a tense meeting, Sarah (technical lead) 
proposed using carbon reduction as the primary KPI.

Maria, a community organizer, responded not by challenging the metric's validity but 
by sharing a story about her grandmother's experience with air quality. As she spoke, 
Sarah began taking notes—not of metrics, but of Maria's grandmother's street name. 
"Wait," Sarah said, "that's three blocks from our monitoring station. What if we..." 
The conversation shifted from defending positions to exploring what neither had 
previously seen: a way to connect technical measurements to lived experience.

This moment illustrates what I call "ontological bridging"—the practice of connecting 
different ways of knowing to create new possibilities. Rather than resolving conflict 
through compromise, actors engage in relational work that transforms what's at stake.
```

## Discussion Section Pattern

### Structure
1. **State finding boldly**: Make the claim (2-3 sentences)
2. **Evidence summary**: What supports it (2-3 sentences)
3. **Theoretical implications**: What this means for theory (3-4 sentences)
4. **Counterintuitive insight**: What's surprising (2-3 sentences)

### Example
```
**Collaboration requires existential, not just coordination, infrastructure.** My 
findings show that actors in successful collaborations systematically build relational 
systems that provide ontological security, moral coherence, and solidarity—work that 
standard coordination mechanisms cannot accomplish.

Across 47 collaborative initiatives, those that sustained productive engagement invested 
heavily in practices I've termed "existential infrastructure building": ontological 
bridging, moral alignment work, and solidarity cultivation. In contrast, initiatives 
that relied solely on coordination mechanisms (clear roles, defined workflows, aligned 
incentives) consistently devolved into destructive conflict.

This challenges dominant theoretical frameworks that treat collaboration as primarily 
a coordination problem (Smith, 2010; Jones, 2012). By showing how micro-relational 
practices constitute the possibility of working together, I reframe collaboration as 
fundamentally about building shared worlds, not just coordinating actions. This shift 
from a coordination to an existential framing opens new theoretical territory for 
understanding how actors solve complex, interdependent problems.

Counterintuitively, this suggests that efforts to improve collaboration by refining 
coordination mechanisms may actually worsen outcomes by distracting from the existential 
work that truly matters. The answer to collaborative failure is not better coordination 
but deeper relational infrastructure.
```

## Implications Template

### Structure
1. **For Practitioners**: Specific, actionable framework
2. **For Scholars**: Research agenda
3. **For Policy**: Institutional design principles

### Example

#### For Practitioners
```
**Build existential infrastructure before coordination structures.** Rather than 
starting collaborative initiatives with workflow design and role definition, invest 
first in practices that build ontological security, moral coherence, and solidarity.

Concretely, this means:
- Create spaces for ontological bridging before problem-solving
- Surface and align moral frameworks before setting objectives  
- Invest in solidarity practices before task assignment

This framework applies across contexts—from healthcare teams coordinating complex 
care to climate coalitions addressing wicked problems to organizations navigating 
change. The specific practices vary, but the principle holds: existential infrastructure 
enables effective coordination, not vice versa.
```

#### For Scholars
```
**Research agenda on relational infrastructure.** This study opens questions about 
how existential infrastructure gets built, maintained, and repaired across different 
contexts. Future research should examine:

- What practices build ontological security in different professional cultures?
- How do actors navigate competing moral frameworks to establish coherence?
- Under what conditions does solidarity emerge or erode?
- How does existential infrastructure scale from dyads to larger collectives?

Methodologically, this requires approaches that can capture micro-relational dynamics 
while tracking their macro-organizational consequences. The framework I've developed 
provides a starting point for such investigation.
```

#### For Policy/Institutional Design
```
**Design for relational work, not just task coordination.** Current policies and 
institutional arrangements optimize for coordination efficiency while neglecting 
existential infrastructure. This generates brittle collaborations that collapse 
under stress.

Institutional designers should:
- Allocate resources for relational infrastructure building
- Create legitimate spaces for existential work
- Evaluate initiatives based on relational health, not just task completion
- Build capacity for ontological bridging, moral alignment, and solidarity cultivation

This shifts from a transaction logic (coordinate to complete tasks) to a relational 
logic (build worlds together).
```
